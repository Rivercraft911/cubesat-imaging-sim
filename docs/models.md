# Orbit and camera models

## Constructed pass

Time zero is the local closest approach to the selected target. The orbit radius is the target's geocentric WGS 84 surface radius plus the configured altitude. Mean motion is `sqrt(GM / a³)`. The model chooses the retrograde inclination that gives first-order circular nodal precession `−(3/2) n J₂ (Rₑ/a)² cos(i)` equal to the mean Sun rate. The ascending or descending branch determines the direction of the pass over the target. [WGS 84 defining parameters](https://earth-info.nga.mil/?action=wgs84&dir=wgs84), [NASA GDC Orbit Primer, p. 4](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/GDC_OrbitPrimer.pdf), [NASA Earth Fact Sheet, J₂](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html)

Pass offset is the signed angular separation from the target to the radial subsatellite point, multiplied by the target's geocentric surface radius. Positive moves the spacecraft to the right of its Earth-fixed ground track. The solver rotates that offset until the target-to-spacecraft vector is perpendicular to Earth-fixed velocity at time zero. Target elevation is geometric above the target's WGS 84 tangent plane. Ground speed is the speed of the radial subsatellite point along the ellipsoid.

The requested epoch selects a UTC date. The model solves the time of closest approach so the node's mean solar time matches LTAN. Frame UTC and apparent Sun direction then advance together. UTC substitutes for UT1, and the model omits nutation, drag, periodic perturbations, refraction and spacecraft flight dynamics. [USNO approximate solar coordinates](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/sun_approx), [USNO approximate sidereal time](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/GAST)

The opening Stanford preset searches 2026 for the strongest alignment between the orbit normal and the Sun while preserving a zero-offset ascending pass. At 550 km it selects March 1 at 01:57:54 UTC, inclination 97.56°, ascending-node mean solar time 18:12 and orbital |β| 89.93°. At closest approach the Sun’s center is 0.074° below Stanford’s geometric horizon. Sampling a full revolution at 121 points puts the subsatellite solar elevation within ±0.3°. The earlier October 3, 18:00 LTAN example still gives a local sunset crossing. Daylight uses a separate plane and selects solar noon on the chosen UTC date. On March 1 that gives 20:21:08 UTC and a +45.23° solar elevation. Both presets put the satellite 89.81° above the local horizon at closest approach. These are constructed comparison opportunities. A flight pass requires orbital elements and their epoch.

As an independent check, JPL Horizons gives airless topocentric solar elevations of −0.080° and +45.228° at the two times above, within 0.01° of both simulations. This checks the solar geometry at Stanford. ## Attitude and optics

ECEF has +X at the Greenwich equator and +Z north. Body +Y is the front and +Z its top. Target tracking, geocentric nadir and Sun tracking define the nominal body +Y aim. Positive look offset rotates the target or nadir aim toward image right before roll. Sun tracking keeps the nominal aim on the Sun. Target tracking projects the target's local tangent north onto the image plane as its roll reference; nadir and Sun modes project Earth's spin axis. Near the north/south grazing singularity, a smooth transition over 0.2–0.1° target elevation limits the roll-reference elevation to 0.1° and transports that frame onto the actual boresight below the horizon, where Earth still occludes the target; this prescribed attitude has no control dynamics or rate limits, and north-up does not imply a level horizon. The main camera is fixed to body +Y, with image right along +X and image down along −Z. Wide A starts with the same axes. Wide B starts at body −Y with image right along −X. Editing either wide camera applies matching optics and local angles to both; the opposite position is a 180° rotation about body +Z. Wide mounts rotate about their local image-down, image-right, then optical-forward axes for yaw, pitch and roll. Quaternions use active `[x, y, z, w]` rotations.

All cameras default to Sony IMX676 color sensors: the recommended 3536 × 3536 recording area with 2 µm pixels. The 3552 × 3552 active array is a different readout area. IMX678 remains available. All cameras use centered optics. Native sensor pixel centers start at `(0.5, 0.5)`. The radial projection maps an off-axis angle `θ` to image radius `f tan(θ)`, `f θ`, or `2 f sin(θ/2)` for rectilinear, equidistant or equisolid lenses. The sensor rectangle, lens image circle and angle cutoff jointly bound usable rays. The 190° default fisheye circle fits the 7.072 mm IMX676 recording height and gives an equidistant focal length of 2.133 mm. These settings and the main 25 mm f/4 lens are design inputs. [Sony IMX676-AACR1, ver. 1.0, p. 2](https://www.sony-semicon.com/files/62/flyer_security/IMX676-AACR1_Flyer.pdf), [Sony IMX678-AAQR1, ver. 2.0, p. 2](https://www.sony-semicon.com/files/62/flyer_security/IMX678-AAQR1_Flyer.pdf), [Sony IMX900-AMR, p. 2](https://www.sony-semicon.com/files/62/flyer_industry/IMX900-AMR_Flyer.pdf), [Raspberry Pi camera hardware](https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/accessories/camera.html)

Center GSD reports separate horizontal and vertical distances between WGS 84 intersections one native pixel apart. Footprint spans are chords along center row and column, clipped at the limb. With the center in space, ground visibility is sampled around the field perimeter and center footprint metrics are unavailable. On the default main camera the center GSD is about 44 m per pixel and the footprint spans about 155.7 × 155.7 km. The entrance pupil diameter is `f/N`, and the ideal circular-pupil Airy diameter is `2.44 λ N` at `λ = 550 nm`. Jitter in pixels is the small-angle pointing standard deviation multiplied by focal length in pixels. Exposure smear is the change in projected position of the midpoint ground intercept over the configured exposure. It follows the selected attitude law and represents center motion. Sensor row timing, measured lens aberrations, terrain relief and radiometric calibration are outside these metrics.

## Image formation and transport

Global textures, a regional satellite basemap, continuous Bay satellite coverage and camera-footprint aerial exports form the imagery levels. GPU mipmaps select the sampled level within each raster; source spacing stays separate from sensor GSD. The 25 and 100 mm opening views use the same Sentinel acquisition. Playback checks coverage at each displayed pass time while network requests run separately. When a finer NAIP survey arrives, the viewfinder and live video blend it in over about 250 ms at normal frame rates. Slow frames extend the transition. As its footprint approaches the raster edge, the entire aerial level tapers toward the satellite base. PNG and frozen comparison captures use the settled blend. Missing aerial coverage reveals the satellite layer, with an inward validity-mask taper. These dated composites retain their photographed illumination. They cannot provide terrain parallax, current weather, calibrated exposure or spatial detail beyond the source raster.

Native images use linear half-float intermediates, a separable nine-sample Gaussian approximation to the Airy core combined with jitter/extra blur, and nine samples of center-field exposure motion. The bounded filter caps sigma at 24 native pixels and smear at 256 pixels and reports either cap. Airy rings, row-dependent rolling shutter and Bayer sampling are omitted. Housing geometry is provisional and is not derived from the optical prescription.

By default, Main and both wide cameras use the same pass lighting. Each ground point uses its local Sun angle, blended night-light emission, atmospheric scattering and cloud coverage. The Bay Area night-light map is a 500 m composite, so a long lens cannot resolve individual lights on campus. **Daylight source reference** preserves the brightness of the original main-camera mosaic for inspecting spatial detail. In **Twilight exposure study**, a unit linear source pixel produces an assumed electron rate at f/4 under overhead sun. The rate scales with source intensity, exposure in milliseconds, `(4 / f-number)²`, and `max(0, sin(solar elevation)) + diffuse fraction` at each ground point. After optical filtering the simulator adds independent noise with variance equal to signal electrons plus read-noise variance, then applies full-well clipping and output gain. Gaussian shot noise loses accuracy at low counts. Diffuse sky, lens transmission, scene radiance and camera sensitivity need measurements for flight brightness predictions. To calibrate a camera and lens, acquire dark-frame pairs and uniform-field exposure pairs with fixed raw settings and known illumination. Use the resulting read noise, saturation and reference response for that configuration. [EMVA 1288 Release 4.0 Linear, §§2.4, 6](https://www.emva.org/wp-content/uploads/EMVA1288Linear_4.0Release.pdf)

Downlink resizes native frames, runs browser WebCodecs encoding, queues actual encoded chunks against an aggregate payload bit rate and delay, then decodes received chunks. Main and panorama share the target rate; ground stitching divides the panorama allocation between two eyes. Queue overflow or interruption drops chunks and suppresses dependent frames until a new keyframe. Exact timestamps pair decoded eyes. Main encoding retains the sensor aspect ratio; IMX676 at a 1080-pixel encode height is 1080 × 1080. Encoded output is limited to 8192 pixels per axis and 16,777,216 pixels per frame before probing codecs. Codec support and measured throughput describe this browser and computer, not flight hardware.

Occupied bandwidth `B` maps to payload rate `B η/(1+α) × (1−r)`. The illustrative DVB-S2 presets use normal 64,800-bit frames without pilots, roll-off `α=0.20` and an additional application reserve `r=0.10`. Bandwidth and throughput controls invert this same relation. This assumes successful demodulation; no waveform, SNR, BER, antenna margin or RF lock is simulated. [ETSI EN 302 307-1 V1.4.1, §5.6 and §6 Table 13, pp. 35–36](https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_en/302300_302399/30230701/01.04.01_60/en_30230701v010401p.pdf)

Auto encoding reserves 20% of payload capacity for bursts, then selects the highest allowed height from 144p–2160p using heuristic bits/pixel/frame targets: AVC 0.12, HEVC/AV1 0.08, VP9 0.10. These are tuning assumptions, not measured quality thresholds. Each stream must fit its share of the rate. Actual encoded chunks still pass through the bounded queue; exceeding capacity can drop frames. Live transport edits preserve bytes already sent; encoder edits settle for 250 ms, retain the last decoded image, and continue the same pass clock.

For a repeatable WebGPU handoff benchmark, run the dev server and open `/benchmarks/handoff.html`. The fixture warms each native frame, alternates readback and direct canvas capture, and checks every output channel. In one local Chromium run the median 1080 × 1080 handoff took 2.1 ms by readback and 0.2 ms with the GPU canvas, across 12 samples per route with identical pixels. These are host handoff times. Encoder latency, GPU completion time and sustained video throughput require separate measurements.
